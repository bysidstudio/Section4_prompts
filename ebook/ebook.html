Chapter 00

Read this first

Most AI-written brand decks fail the same way: every page sounds like it came from the same machine, and any page could be pasted into a competitor's deck. This playbook is the antidote.

It documents a set of prompts that walks one client through Section 04 of a Brand Strategy Presentation: Purpose, Vision, Values, Positioning, Value Proposition, Personality, Voice, Key Messages and a one-page Summary. A tenth prompt, the Strategy Check, audits the finished work before Creative Direction starts.

Who this is for

A solo brand designer who sells brand strategy as part of the job and wants a repeatable way to produce it. You are the decision maker. The prompts do the strategic reasoning, offer defensible routes and write client-ready copy. They never decide for you.

What it assumes you already have

  • A Brand Discovery Brief and Evidence from the client. Everything downstream is built from these. No brief, no strategy.
  • Earlier approved pages. Positioning leans on 2.1 Target Audience, 2.2 Audience Insights, 2.3 Customer Journey, 3.1 Competitor Analysis, 3.2 Positioning Map and 3.3 Market Opportunity. Those pages are produced by earlier steps in the wider presentation. This playbook covers Section 04 and the check that follows.
  • A Brand Strategy Presentation template with fixed boxes for each field, and a Decisions document where you log each choice.
  • One long, continuous chat with an AI assistant. This matters more than anything else here. See Chapter 01.
Mentor note

This playbook will not rescue a thin brief. The prompts are written to stop and say what is missing rather than invent filler. When they stop, believe them. Go back to the client.

One naming correction

Anywhere you see "1.2 Business Goals", read it as 1.2 Brand Goals.

Chapter 01

How the system works

One chat, not nine

You paste the Brand Discovery Brief and Evidence once, near the top of the conversation. Then you run each page prompt (4.1, 4.2, 4.3 and so on) as a new turn in the same chat. Every later prompt reads the brief, the evidence and every earlier approved page straight out of the chat history. Nothing is re-pasted.

This is what makes the pages agree with each other. Purpose feeds Vision. Values feed Positioning. Positioning feeds Value Proposition, and so on down the chain.

The loop on every page

  1. Run the prompt. It returns one route or two, each with the page's exact fields filled in, plus a one-sentence "My lean".
  2. Pick. Take one route whole, or mix pieces from both.
  3. Tell the chat what you used. Type Using Route 1 or Using Route 2, or say which piece came from where. If you skip this step, later pages cannot see your decision.
  4. Paste into the presentation. Each field goes into its fixed box.
  5. Run the two quick checks printed at the bottom of every output.
  6. Log it. One line in the Decisions document: "4.x Page Name. I chose ______ because ______."
  7. Move to the next page.

If a route does not fit, tell the assistant what to change. It rewrites that route only. You do not start again.

Routes: one or two, never padded

Each prompt first decides whether the evidence supports one strong route or two genuinely different ones. It is told not to force a second route to look balanced. A second route only appears when it would lead to a different understanding of the business and different decisions downstream. Under each route, "Choose this if" gives a reason to pick it, never a knock on the other.

When two routes are strong, the output ends with a one-sentence lean. When they are tied, it names the single question you need to answer to break the tie.

Already confirmed means done

If you have already typed "Using Route X" for a page, re-running that page's prompt gets one short acknowledgement and nothing else. The system will not re-argue a decision you have made.

Chapter 02

The rules every page shares

Every page prompt carries the same spine. Understanding it saves you from fighting the output.

1. Source rule: nothing from nowhere

Each page uses only the brief, the evidence and earlier approved pages. No outside knowledge, no invented facts, capabilities or ambitions. The assistant must not silently override an approved decision. It flags conflicts instead. If the chat genuinely lacks what a page needs, it says exactly what is missing and stops.

2. Fresh writing

Earlier pages are used for facts and consistency of meaning, never for sentences, openings or phrasing. A deck where every page sounds generated is exactly what the system exists to avoid.

3. Plain voice

Write for the client, as you would say it to the founder across a table. Short sentences, mostly under 20 words and never over 30. One idea each. Active voice. No em dashes. No hedging words such as "may", "might", "aims to", "strives to". A long list of tired words is banned (full list in the Appendix), and the word "space" is never used for a category.

Weak
a simplified and accessible approach.
Strong
Five products. Each one does a different job. You do not have to work out the order.

4. Hard word limits

Every field has a target range and a hard maximum. Fields are pasted into fixed boxes in the deck, so going over breaks the layout. The low end of a target is a direction, not a rule: a sharp line a few words short beats one padded with filler.

Be honest about this

A language model cannot guarantee an exact word count from an instruction alone. The prompts say so themselves: you verify the actual count before pasting. Do not skip this. Chapter 03 explains how.

5. The silent check

Before answering, each prompt runs a private checklist (is it specific to this business, does it trace to evidence, is anything over its maximum, are the two routes truly different) and rewrites anything that fails. The check is never shown. You only see the result, which is why your own two quick checks at the end of each page matter.

6. Swap tests

The recurring test across the whole set is the swap. Replace the brand name with a competitor's. If the sentence still works, it is too generic. Positioning, Values, Taglines and the Strategy Check all use this same test.

Page 4.1 · Step 1

Brand Purpose

Why the business exists beyond making money.

The job

Purpose is the enduring reason the business needs to exist, beyond what it currently sells. The assistant infers it by connecting the strongest signals: the founder's reason for starting, the customer's problem, the business reality, the market opportunity and any approved strategy. It must not just restate the founder's words or the customer's problem.

Three tests it must pass

  • Range test. It must still hold if the product or service range changed substantially. If it only describes what they sell today, it is a description, not a purpose.
  • Swap test. If it would fit several other businesses in the category, it is wrong.
  • Founder-story test. If removing the founder's name leaves a repeated anecdote instead of a business reason, it is repetition, not synthesis. Rewrite it.

Banned openers: "to empower people to...", "to inspire...", "to revolutionise...", "to make the world a better place...", "to help people be their best selves...".

What This Rules Out

This is the field that keeps Purpose honest. It must name something real the business would turn down because of its purpose, even when refusing costs money. The assistant is told to work out the genuine refusal first and write the purpose it follows from. If no genuine refusal exists, the purpose is too vague, and it must not fabricate one.

Weak
This rules out compromising on quality. (Nobody would claim the opposite, so it rules out nothing.)
Strong
A refusal someone actually has to make, at a real cost to the business.

Routes

RouteBuilt from
1 · The Founder's ReasonWhat they saw, what frustrated them, what they wanted to change.
2 · The Customer's ProblemThe specific frustration the business exists to remove.

Two routes appear only if they lead to a different understanding of why the business exists and to different refusals.

How You Will See It is a concrete, pointable instance of the purpose in the real business: a product, packaging, service, process or customer experience. It is not a list of values and not visual direction.

Out of scope here

No tagline (that is 4.8), no values (4.3), no vision (4.2). Do not let purpose slide into a value proposition or inflate the business beyond the evidence.

Your two checks

  • Would this purpose still be true if the product or service range changed completely?
  • Swap in another business's name. Does it still work unchanged? If yes, sharpen it.
Page 4.2 · Step 2

Brand Vision

Where the business is going, and what would prove it got there.

The job

A vision describes the future the business is trying to create: what success looks like once the business has become what the founder wants it to become. It is not today's business, not a purpose, not positioning, not a value proposition and not a tagline. It builds on the approved 4.1 Purpose and must never rewrite or contradict it.

The year rule

The statement must open "By [year], [BRAND] will...". The year comes from the client's stated ambition in the brief, either directly or calculated from a stated period such as "three to five years". If the brief gives no timeframe at all, the assistant says so and stops. It does not invent a year.

Not a sales target

"Reach one million in sales" is a goal, not a vision. The question is what the business will have become when it gets there.

Weak
By 2030, we will hit ten million in revenue.
Strong
By 2030, [BRAND] is the brand people name when a friend asks what actually worked.

Milestones must be checkable

Each milestone has to let the founder look back and answer yes or no. Numbers and dates appear only where the brief provides them. With no numbers, a milestone can still be checkable through a concrete development such as a new market, a named retail presence, a specific launch or a defined product expansion. If the evidence supports only two credible milestones, the assistant says so rather than manufacturing a third.

Not checkable
Increased brand awareness.
Checkable
Stocked in at least three national retailers.

Routes

RouteBuilt from
1 · Where The Business Gets ToFuture position, reach, scale, markets, retail presence, recognition or role.
2 · What Changes Because Of ItWhat becomes different for customers, people served or the category because the business succeeds.

Both must come from the client's actual ambition. Route 2 must not be made more ambitious just because it sounds more inspiring, and inferred social impact is not turned into a formal ambition unless the brief supports it.

Your two checks

  • Does every number and date come from the client? If one appeared from nowhere, tell the assistant and it removes it.
  • Could you check each milestone later and get a clear yes or no?
Page 4.3 · Step 3

Core Values

Five things the business will not compromise on.

Why this page fails most often

Core Values collapses into lists like Quality, Integrity, Innovation. The prompt is built to prevent that with a reversed method, run silently:

  1. Write the Rules Out line first: a specific thing this business refuses to do, grounded in approved evidence.
  2. Then name the one-word value that refusal comes from.
  3. Then write what that value means for this brand.

Never start from the word. You cannot write a specific refusal for a generic word, so a generic word fails before it gets to step 1.

Banned values

Quality, Integrity, Innovation, Excellence, Passion, Authenticity, Transparency, Trust, Respect, Commitment, Customer-first, and Simplicity on its own. They are banned because no business would claim the opposite, so they say nothing. If the evidence points toward one of these ideas, the assistant must find the specific behaviour underneath and name that. Swapping in a generic synonym is also not allowed.

The Rules Out test

For every line ask: could this be written about a competitor? If yes, the value is generic.

Weak
Rules out cutting corners on ingredients. (Any brand can say this.)
Strong
Rules out launching a product we cannot explain in one line on the label. (A real decision someone has to make.)

The strongest lines describe a real trade-off: what the business refuses even when saying yes would be easier, cheaper or commercially attractive.

Values are a system

The five should show distinct behaviours or decision boundaries, not five versions of one principle. Values must not repeat the Purpose or Vision, and must not become personality traits. Ask: what decisions would this business make differently because this value exists? If nothing, it is filler. If the evidence honestly supports fewer than five, the assistant says so and stops.

Routes

RouteBuilt from
1 · How They Make ThingsProduct, craft, standards, process, what goes in and what stays out.
2 · How They Treat PeopleCustomers, service, honesty, communication, what the business will and will not say or do with people.

Two routes only if at least three of the five values are genuinely different between the sets.

Fields and limits (for each of five values)

FieldFormatHard max
ValueONE word. Not two. Not a phrase.1

Your two checks

  • Read the five value words alone, without meanings. Could they sit on a competitor's page? If yes, ask for replacements.
  • Does every value trace to something the client said or to a clearly supported inference? A value invented to fill the page is worse than an honest gap.
Page 4.4 · Step 4

Brand Positioning

What the brand should be known for, relative to the alternatives.

The job

This is the central market decision in Section 04: what should this brand own in the customer's mind? The position must connect who the brand is for, what that customer needs, what competitors already own, what gap exists, and what this business can credibly deliver. Positioning is not a description of the business, and an empty competitive space is not automatically an opportunity.

Earn the position

A position must pass three connected tests: customer need, competitive gap and business capability. If one is missing, the position is not defensible yet.

Inputs are unusually strict

Customer and market pages (2.x, 3.x) are the primary inputs. Purpose, Vision and Values give context and consistency only. They must never introduce a position the customer, competitor and market evidence does not support. The assistant uses only the three confirmed competitors from 3.1 and 3.2, same names, same order, and does no outside research on them.

The statement shape

For [AUDIENCE] who [NEED/TENSION], [BRAND] is the [CATEGORY]
that [DISTINCTIVE POSITION] because [KEY DIFFERENTIATOR].

This is a strategic structure, not a copywriting template. Audience comes from 2.1, the need from 2.2, the position from 3.1 to 3.3. The differentiator must be something the business can actually support today.

Proof

A proof must be something the business already has: a product, process, system, documented capability or demonstrated way of working. Vague claims ("a commitment to quality", "customer-focused service") are out unless the evidence makes the specific behaviour clear. Do not force three proof points. The strongest goes in Why It's Different.

Routes

RouteWhere the difference lives
1 · Different In What It DoesThe offer itself: mechanic, system, process, product structure, service model. The belief follows from the offer.
2 · Different In What It BelievesA clear point of view that challenges or reframes how the category normally works. The offer is the evidence for the belief.

Two routes only if the brand would behave differently and the Position to Own itself would differ.

The words "best", "leading", "unique" and "premium" are banned unless the evidence gives them a specific, defensible meaning.

Your two checks

  • Cover the brand name and read the statement aloud. Could a competitor's name replace it and still make sense? If yes, it is too generic.
  • Is every proof or differentiator something the business already has? If it is aspirational, it is not proof.
Page 4.5 · Step 5

Value Proposition

What the customer gets from choosing the brand.

The job

Positioning says what the brand should be known for. This page translates that position into a clear customer promise: what they get, what follows from it, why to believe it, and why to choose this brand over the alternatives. It must not repeat the positioning statement, become a feature list, or make every benefit another version of the Promise.

The Promise: one commitment

One clear commitment. Not a list. Not three things joined with "and".

Weak
Quality products, great service and honest pricing. (Three promises, so none.)
Strong
Every product does one job, and the label tells you which.

Four benefits

Each is a distinct customer gain that follows from the Promise: practical, meaningful, supported by approved audience insights. Not four features, and not the Promise reworded. The assistant does not invent a fourth just to fill the box.

Reason to Believe

This is where the Promise stops being marketing. Use real evidence: a product, process, system, documented fact or demonstrated way of working. If the evidence is weak, the assistant says so plainly instead of writing something that sounds convincing.

Not evidence
We are committed to quality.
Evidence
Every formula is tested for eight weeks before it ships, and the results go on the pack.

Why Choose Us

Answers "why this brand instead of the alternatives?" It uses the competitor analysis and the approved position, and does not just summarise the benefits. No claim of superiority without evidence.

Routes

RouteThe Promise focuses on
1 · The OutcomeWhat the customer ends up with: the result, change or state they reach.
2 · The ExperienceWhat it is like getting there: ease, effort, process or fit into the customer's life.

Both can be true. The designer is choosing which to lead with. Two routes appear only if the Promise and at least three of four benefits differ, and the client's website would say meaningfully different things.

Page 4.6 · Step 6

Brand Personality

How the brand behaves.

The job

Personality is not a list of attractive adjectives. It is the set of human qualities that consistently shape how the brand behaves, presents itself and feels in use. It must follow from the approved strategy, never from the designer's personal taste. Positioning (4.4) and Value Proposition (4.5) are the direct inputs. Core Values define which behaviours are allowed and rejected.

Traits and their boundaries

Five traits, each paired in order with a Never Become: the close cousin of the trait, what it turns into when pushed too far or handled badly.

Good pairs
Confident, never smug.
Warm, never soft.
Direct, never blunt.
Calm, never dull.
Bad pair
Confident, never boring. (Boring is not the failure mode of confidence.)

Where the client listed words to avoid in the brief, they are used where they create a meaningful boundary. The pairs must form one coherent behavioural system, not five positives with five unrelated negatives.

Visually and Verbally

Both lines must be actionable constraints. "Clean and modern" and "friendly and clear" give no direction. Direction is welcome, but this is not a creative direction system: no exact fonts, palettes, layouts or photography systems unless the approved strategy requires it.

Archetypes come last

The Primary Archetype is developed after the traits and boundaries, as shorthand for what is already there, never the other way round. The assistant picks the archetype that fits the evidence, not the one that sounds most attractive. If none is defensible, it writes N/A and says why.

A Secondary Archetype is optional and stays N/A by default. It is added only when the Primary cannot resolve a real tension: one group of traits pulls toward one archetype and a different, equally evidenced group pulls toward another. The test: name the specific traits the Primary fails to account for, and check the Secondary accounts for exactly those. If you cannot name the split, leave it N/A.

Routes

The prompt suggests example directions only (for instance Leads With Certainty versus Leads With Warmth) and warns against forcing them. Two routes appear only if at least three traits differ, the Never Become boundaries change meaningfully, and the Visually and Verbally lines instruct differently.

Page 4.7 · Step 7

Brand Voice

How the personality sounds in writing.

The job

Voice is the writing behaviour of the approved 4.6 Personality. Positioning and Value Proposition decide what the brand needs to communicate. The client's own words from Brief Section 08 (Client Voice) keep the result believable.

Translate, don't repeat

Do not just copy personality words into voice principles. If the personality says direct, show how directness appears in language. If it says warm, show how warmth appears in language.

Use the founder's words as evidence, not copy

Look for patterns in Section 08: sentence style, directness, word choice, formality, how they explain the business, how they talk about customers. Never copy their sentences into the output. If a proposed principle clearly contradicts how the founder naturally speaks, reconsider it. The goal is a usable voice that feels believable for this business, not a copy of the founder.

Principles are writing rules

Each principle must tell a writer what to do. Keep the principle name short and put the instruction in the Meaning.

Not useful
Clear: we communicate clearly.
Useful
Clear: one idea per sentence, and explain unfamiliar terms plainly.

One voice, shifting tone

The five principles apply everywhere. The four channel boxes (Social, Website, Email, In Person) describe small shifts in delivery, not different voices. If Social and Website read like two different brands, that is two voices. Rewrite them.

Words We Avoid

Six words that go to whoever writes the client's website. They must be specific to this brand: words that conflict with the personality or positioning, make the brand sound like its competitors, or are overused in the category. The client's own avoid-words from the brief come first when still appropriate. Generic consultant jargon is a waste of the list.

Routes

Example directions only: How It Explains (the writing removes confusion) and How It Reassures (the writing makes the customer feel safe choosing). Two routes only if at least three principles differ and a copywriter would write a meaningfully different product page while the brand still sounds like the same brand.

Page 4.8 · Step 8

Key Messages

What the brand actually says out loud.

The job

This is the first page where the strategy becomes customer-facing language. It expresses the approved strategy and creates no new one. The direct inputs are 4.4, 4.5, 4.6 and 4.7, with Audience Insights and Customer Journey deciding what should be said and what action makes sense.

Taglines: develop many, show one

The assistant privately develops several genuinely different directions: the brand's point of view, its distinctive promise, the unusual way it solves the problem, the customer's desired shift, or the Position to Own. It rejects any that turn generic, motivational, descriptive or interchangeable, and outputs only the strongest survivor. You never see candidates or scoring.

Lines that mainly describe the category or state a generic benefit are rejected. Examples of failure patterns: "Better [category]", "[Category], made simple", "Made for [audience]", "Designed for life", "Simply better", "Made with care", "Built for you", "Feel the difference", "Do more", "Less is more".

Five tests, all must pass

TestThe question
1 · OwnabilityCould a competitor use it unchanged?
2 · Specific associationWhat exact idea does a customer link to this brand? If it is only "quality", "care", "confidence", "simplicity" or "innovation", reject.
3 · Strategic traceCan you point to an approved idea in Positioning, Value Proposition, Personality or Position to Own that it compresses?
4 · Category distanceDoes it separate the brand from normal category language?
5 · MemorabilityShort, natural, distinctive and clear without a paragraph of explanation?

Two further tests: cover-up (cover the brand name, would it still read as a generic category statement?) and reverse (what would the brand be giving up if it stopped standing for this idea? "Nothing specific" means rewrite). No pun or rhyme is required. A plain line with a specific idea beats a clever line with nothing ownable behind it.

The honest exit

If no candidate passes all five tests, the assistant does not invent one. It says the approved strategy does not yet contain a distinctive idea strong enough to support a credible tagline, and stops. Treat that as a finding about your strategy, not a malfunction.

One-Liner

Answers "what does this brand do?" in plain language the founder can say aloud without sounding like they are reading a strategy document. It does not repeat the Positioning Statement and is not a slogan.

Lead With, Then Prove, Then Ask

These form a chain, not a list. Lead With earns attention. Then Prove removes the doubt Lead With creates. Then Ask gives a clear next action. Check the chain: what does Lead With make the customer believe, what doubt could that create, does Then Prove answer that doubt, and once resolved, does Then Ask make sense? The doubt must come from approved Audience Insights, not an invented objection. Avoid defaulting to "Discover more", "Learn more" or "Get started" unless the context makes them meaningful.

Routes

Example directions: Lead With The Problem (name the frustration, then introduce the brand) and Lead With The Difference (open with what makes the brand different, then connect it to the problem). Two routes only if the Tagline and Lead With truly differ and the client's homepage would open differently.

Page 4.9 · Step 9

Brand Strategy Summary

All approved decisions on one page.

The job

This is the final page. It has no routes: every strategic decision was made earlier, so you get one summary, not two versions. It runs once, after every prior page is approved.

Nothing new

Every field must come from an approved page, or from a direct combination of approved ideas whose meaning does not change. The assistant may shorten and simplify. It may not add a new claim, promise, positioning, audience definition, trait or voice principle. A phrase it cannot trace back is removed. It may not strengthen claims either: "plans to" does not become a present-tense fact, and an aspiration does not become an established capability.

If approved pages disagree

While compressing, the assistant may discover that two approved pages conflict, such as a Personality that does not match the Voice, or a Promise that conflicts with the Positioning. It must not soften the summary and must not silently pick a side. It names the two pages, explains the conflict briefly, and gives the summary anyway using the approved content as written. You then fix the original page, rather than having the problem hidden in the summary.

Heads up

The One-Liner is 25 to 30 words on 4.8 but 20 to 25 on 4.9, and Reason to Believe is 30 to 35 on 4.5 but 20 to 25 here. So the Summary version is a shortened form. When it must stay true to the approved wording, accuracy beats novelty: keep an approved phrase if it is the clearest expression of the decision.

When 4.9 is approved, the next step is the Strategy Check.

Final gate

The Strategy Check

Is Sections 01 to 04 ready for Creative Direction?

A different kind of prompt

The page prompts write brand copy. The Strategy Check does not. You export the Brand Strategy Presentation to PDF and upload it. The assistant reads only Sections 01 to 04 and reports high-impact problems. It deliberately ignores Section 05 and anything after it, even if it is complete, blank or from another brand. The latest PDF is the source of truth, not old drafts and not what you meant to write.

What it looks for

Problem typeExample
ContradictionPersonality vs Positioning, Voice vs Personality, Vision vs Purpose, Key Messages communicating a different idea than Positioning.
Weak differentiationThe brand does not separate from the three approved competitors in a way that matters.
Unsupported claimInvented proof, capabilities, milestones or market facts. Reasonable inference is allowed. The rule: don't invent, do infer when supported.
Strategic repetitionReason to Believe repeating the Promise, benefits restating the Promise, the Summary introducing a new idea.
High-impact genericnessAn idea that could move to another brand with only the name changed, and that materially weakens the strategy.

The filter that keeps it useful

Before reporting anything, the assistant asks whether fixing it would materially change the strategy, customer interpretation, positioning, claims, communication or Creative Direction. If not, it is ignored: wording preferences, punctuation, layout, small word-count differences. This is explicitly not a perfection audit.

On the first check it reports at most three issues, aiming for fewer. One real problem gets one report. Zero gets zero. If several symptoms share one cause, they are combined.

When the right fix is restoring an earlier approved decision, the replacement names its source page and field explicitly, so it does not look like new strategy. When there is a real problem but not enough evidence to write a safe fix, the assistant says "Do not rewrite this yet", states exactly what evidence is missing from Sections 01 to 04, and says what you need to verify. It never invents a replacement.

When two approved decisions conflict, it preserves whichever existing decision best fits the overall chain and recommends the smallest correction. It never invents a third direction.

The recheck

After you make only the listed changes, export a new PDF and upload it in the same chat. The earlier report is a checklist and the new PDF is the truth. Each previous issue is marked FIXED or STILL OPEN. It reports full detail only for still-open issues, plus any genuinely new high-impact problem your changes caused. It never reopens fixed issues and never goes looking for optional improvements.

When everything is resolved, it ends with:

STRATEGY CHECK: CLEAR
All previous fixes are complete.
No new high-impact issues found in Sections 01 to 04.
CLEARED FOR CREATIVE DIRECTION.

Then it stops. The goal is not perfect wording. It is a coherent, differentiated, evidence-supported foundation for Creative Direction.

Chapter 03

Where this system can fail

A playbook that only sells you the system is useless. These are the real weak points, taken from how the prompts are built, and what to do about each.

1. Word counts are not guaranteed

The prompts state this themselves. A hard maximum in an instruction is a request, not an enforcement. Fix: count every field before pasting. Paste the output into a word counter or use a quick script. Over the max means ask the assistant to cut it, not "trim it yourself and move on", because the shorter version has to keep its meaning.

2. Everything depends on one long chat

The design assumes the brief, evidence and all approved pages stay readable in the conversation. If you start a new chat, trim history, or the conversation grows long enough for earlier content to be summarised or dropped, later pages lose their inputs and can drift or contradict earlier decisions. Fix: keep the Decisions document as your own record. If you must start a new chat, re-paste the brief, the evidence and every approved page before running the next prompt.

3. "Using Route X" is easy to forget

The next page only knows what you decided if you said so in the chat. A forgotten confirmation means the assistant may build on a route you did not choose. Fix: make the confirmation the first thing you do after pasting into the deck, every time.

4. You can approve a weak page and bury the problem

Because every later page treats approved pages as settled, an early mistake compounds. A vague Purpose produces vague Values, which produce a generic Position. Fix: apply the Rules Out test seriously on 4.1 and 4.3. Those two fields are the earliest filter for generic thinking. The Strategy Check catches contradictions and generic ideas late. Do not rely on it to rescue early pages.

5. A stop is information, not an error

The prompts will stop and say what is missing: no timeframe for the Vision, fewer than five honest Values, no tagline that passes the tests. The temptation is to coax something out anyway. Don't. Fix: take the missing item back to the client, or accept the gap.

6. Two routes do not mean two good options

A second route appears only when the evidence supports one, but you should still check. If both routes read as the same idea in different words, say so and ask for a single route. The prompts' own test is whether the designer would make meaningfully different decisions depending on the route.

7. Earlier-section pages are outside this repo

4.4 to 4.8 rely on pages 2.1 to 3.3, and 4.9 relies on 2.1. Those prompts are not part of the Section 04 set. If those pages are weak, Section 04 inherits the weakness. Check them before you start.

8. Your own review is the last defence

The assistant's silent self-check is hidden by design. You cannot audit it. Use the two quick checks at the end of every page, and treat the Strategy Check as the final gate, not the first time you read your own work critically.

B. Words and phrases the assistant will not write

Words: empower, elevate, unlock, resonate, seamless, holistic, curated, bespoke, leverage, robust, ecosystem, landscape, solution, offering, journey, transformative, innovative, disruptive, best-in-class, cutting-edge, value-add, synergy, streamline, optimise, consumer, demographic, authentic, passionate, dedicated.

Phrases: "committed to excellence", "more than just a", "at its core", "in today's market", "we don't just X, we Y".

Hedges: may, might, could potentially, arguably, aims to, seeks to, strives to, tends to, it is worth noting.

Style: no em dashes. Say "people" or "customers", never "consumers". Never call a category a "space".
